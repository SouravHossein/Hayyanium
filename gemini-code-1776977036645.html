Chemical News & Journal of Physical Science · London · 1866

The Law of Octaves

On the Relations of the Equivalent Numbers of the Elements

John Alexander Reina Newlands, F.C.S.

"The numbers of analogous elements generally differ either by 7 or by some multiple of seven; in other words, members of the same group stand to each other in the same relation as the extremities of one or more octaves in music."

— J. A. R. Newlands, read before the Chemical Society, March 1866

The Musical Analogy

Newlands observed that if all known elements be arranged by increasing atomic weight and assigned an ordinal number, every eighth element shares the physical and chemical character of the first — precisely as the eighth note of a musical scale echoes the first.

He was the first person to assign ordinal numbers to the elements — what we now call atomic numbers — perceiving periodicity as a numerical law rather than mere resemblance.

The analogy holds beautifully from Hydrogen through Calcium; beyond that point it breaks under the weight of heavier elements whose spacing no longer fits so neat a rule.

Critical Anomalies

  • Beyond Calcium (17): The octave pattern collapses for all heavier elements; properties no longer recur at intervals of seven.
  • Forced Pairs: Co & Ni, Pt & Ir, Ba & V, Ce & La, Di & Mo, Ro & Ru occupy single slots — an admission the sequence cannot accommodate all elements.
  • No Noble Gases: He, Ne, Ar, and others remained undiscovered; the table has no Group 18 column.
  • Iron Misplaced: Fe shares a row with O and S — a non-metal group — while the similar Co & Ni sit beside the halogens.

Historical Reception

Presented to the Chemical Society in March 1866, the table was met with mockery. Prof. G. C. Foster asked Newlands whether he had examined the elements by their initial letters.

After Mendeleev's periodic law triumphed, the Royal Society awarded Newlands the Davy Medal in 1887 for the prior discovery of periodicity — five years after Mendeleev and Meyer received the same honour.

Note on orientation: In Newlands' original, groups of analogous elements run horizontally (across rows) and periods run vertically (down columns) — the inverse of the modern periodic table convention.

Di (Didymium): Not a true element. Later resolved by C. A. von Welsbach (1885) into Praseodymium and Neodymium.

Prediction of Germanium (1864): Before this table, Newlands predicted an undiscovered element of atomic weight ~73 analogous to Si, Ti, and Sn — confirmed when Winkler isolated germanium in 1886.
